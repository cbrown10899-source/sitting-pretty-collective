What color season am I?

Twelve palettes, sorted by how light you are, how bright, and how warm. Below is every one of them with hex codes, metals and the tests that separate the ones people mix up. Start with the family that looks closest and work inward.

Read three properties, not a name

Guessing at a season name is where most people go wrong. Sort yourself by the three underlying properties instead, in this order — each one narrows twelve down fast.

Value
Are you at your best in light colors or deep ones?
Chroma
Do clear bright colors suit you, or grayed muted ones?
Temperature
Gold or silver at the wrist — which looks cleaner?

Temperature is the last question, not the first. It is the property people most often get wrong, and it is far easier to judge once value and chroma have already ruled most of the palettes out.

Where people go wrong

  • Judging one color at a time instead of two side by side — the eye needs a comparison to see a difference
  • Choosing colors you like over colors that suit you
  • Assuming dark hair means Winter — depth of hair is not the same as depth of coloring
  • Trusting one photo taken in the wrong light (see below — it matters more than anything else)

How to take the photo

Chroma can only read what the camera captured. A photo shot in the wrong light, or through a filter, bakes in a color cast that outweighs the subtle shifts the read depends on — so this one step changes the result more than anything else you can do. It takes about five minutes.

  1. Use indirect daylightFace a window with natural light, or step into open shade outside; an overcast sky is ideal. Avoid direct sun, which casts warm and throws hard shadows, and avoid golden hour, which turns everything orange.
  2. Turn off every other lightLamps and ceiling bulbs each add their own tint — incandescent runs yellow, most LEDs run cool — and mixed light is the hardest of all to correct. Daylight only.
  3. Drape white under your chinHold a plain sheet of white paper, a white towel, or a white shirt so it covers your shoulders and chest. It blocks color bouncing up from your clothing, and it gives the camera a true white to balance against.
  4. Bare face, hair backTake off makeup and pull your hair away from your face. You are reading skin — not products, not hair color.
  5. No filters, no beauty modeSwitch off beauty, HDR and auto-enhance, and apply no filter. Those features quietly adjust the exact tones you are trying to measure.
  6. Check the white, then shoot a fewLook at the paper in each shot. If it reads truly white, the light is neutral and the photo can be trusted. If it looks yellow or blue, move and retake.
The white paper is your reference. It is the one thing in the frame whose true color you already know. If it reads clean white on screen your lighting is right; if it carries a tint, so does everything else in the photo — including your skin.
